## Section 2: What goes wrong in an empty home (1:15-3:08)
> Let us start with the problem. A home that is lived in gets noticed. Someone hears the dripping tap, sees the damp patch, smells the drain. An empty home has nobody to notice. So small problems grow quietly.
>
> The first one is water. A slow leak under a sink, or behind a toilet, can run for weeks. And taps and drains that are never used can dry out. That lets smells come back up from the pipes.
>
> The second is damp and mould. Cyprus is sunny, but winter can bring proper rain. A closed-up house with no airflow can get condensation on walls, in wardrobes, and on mattresses. Treating damp properly means finding the cause, not just painting over it.
>
> The third is sun and heat. Long summers are hard on exterior sealant, shutters, paintwork and terrace surfaces. Roof waterproofing takes a beating. It is far better to find a small crack in September than a leak in January. Sealing a small crack early is a simple repair. Waiting for the rain is not.
>
> The fourth is pests. Insects, birds nesting in vents, and sometimes rodents. Not every house, but it is common enough that you should look for it.
>
> The fifth is how the property looks from outside. An overgrown garden and a full postbox tell everyone the place is empty. A tidy house sends a quiet message that someone is looking after it.
>
> None of these is dramatic on day one. The cost comes from time. That is why a routine matters more than any single fix.

## Section 3: What a good visit should cover (3:08-4:44)
> So what does a good maintenance visit actually cover? Here is the list we suggest. You can adapt it.
>
> One. Look before you touch anything. Take dated photos of every room. Look for stains, smells, and anything out of place.
>
> Two. Run the water. Open every tap and flush every toilet, so the drains hold water again and any leak shows itself. Check under sinks, and around the water heater and the tank.
>
> Three. Check the electrics sensibly. Look at the main switchboard for tripped switches, test the lights, and check the fridge if it is left running. No opening panels, and no DIY. If something looks wrong, a qualified electrician needs to look at it.
>
> Four. Air the house. Open the windows for a while. Let air move through wardrobes and cupboards, and check mattresses and soft furnishings.
>
> Five. The outside. The roof, as far as you can see it from the ground. Gutters and drains, terrace, balcony, gate, pool equipment if you have a pool, and the garden.
>
> Six. Send a report. Photos, a short written note, and a simple list: what is urgent, what can wait, and what to keep an eye on. You should never have to chase anyone to find out what happened. That is how we like to work. Clear communication, every time.

## Section 4: Keys, access and communication (5:06-6:27)
> Access is the part owners worry about most, and fair enough. You are handing someone the key to your home, so choose carefully.
>
> Ask a few questions. Are they registered and insured? Can they show examples of previous work? Who else will hold a key, and where are the keys stored? Good practice is to label keys with a code, not your address.
>
> Put the arrangement in writing. It should say how often visits happen, what is included, and what happens in an emergency. If you want advice on the contract itself, please speak to your own Cyprus lawyer.
>
> Then agree how you will communicate. Time zones matter. Decide in advance what counts as an emergency, and what can be fixed without asking you. For example, you might say anything up to [ADD AMOUNT, e.g. EUR X] can be repaired straight away, and anything above that needs your approval by message.
