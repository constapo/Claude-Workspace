## Section 2: Why Cyprus houses get damp (0:50-1:40)
On-screen text: "Concrete frame + brick infill + little insulation = cold walls". Simple diagram of warm air meeting a cold wall.
B-roll: Exterior shots of typical Cyprus houses and apartment blocks; interior shot of a cold north-facing corner; steam from a kettle or shower; clothes drying indoors.

> Why is this so common here? Most Cyprus homes have a reinforced concrete frame with brick or block walls, often with little or no insulation and older windows. In summer, that is fine. In winter, the inside surface of those walls gets cold, especially on north-facing sides and in corners. At the same time, we cook, we shower, we dry washing indoors, and some of us use portable gas heaters, which release a lot of moisture. Warm, damp air touches a cold wall and turns into water. That is condensation, one of the most common causes of winter mould here. Local knowledge matters, because a coastal home and a hill-village home do not behave the same way.

## Section 3: Three kinds of damp and how to tell them apart (1:40-2:35)
On-screen text: "1. Condensation 2. Penetrating damp 3. Rising damp". Show each with a one-line description as it is spoken.
B-roll: Close-ups of each type: fine black spotting; a stained patch with a tide-mark edge on a ceiling or wall; flaking plaster low on an older stone wall with white salt deposits. Use your own footage, with permission from the property owner.

> Here is a useful clue. If the mould follows a straight line, like the edge of a ceiling or the shape of a column, you are probably looking at a thermal bridge. Concrete columns and beams stay colder than the brick around them, so moisture collects there first. Condensation is usually fine black or grey spotting. It wipes off, and it gets worse on cold, still days. Penetrating damp is different. It is a patch, often with a darker edge, and it gets worse after rain. And rising damp, mostly a problem in older stone houses, shows as staining and flaking plaster low on the wall, sometimes with white, powdery salts. If you are not sure which one you have, that is completely normal. Telling them apart is exactly what we do on a site visit.

## Section 4: Permanent fixes for condensation (2:55-3:50)
On-screen text: "Moisture. Air. Temperature." Then a numbered list of fixes.
B-roll: Extractor fan venting outside; a window opened briefly; a dehumidifier running; wardrobe pulled slightly away from a wall; external insulation being installed (if you have footage) or an illustration.

> Fixing condensation comes down to three things: moisture, air and temperature. First, reduce moisture at the source. Use extractor fans in the bathroom and kitchen that vent outside, put lids on pans, and dry washing in a ventilated space. Second, ventilate. Open the windows fully for ten to fifteen minutes each day, even in winter, rather than leaving them barely open for hours. Third, keep temperatures steady. A dehumidifier and gentle, constant heating usually work better than short bursts of strong heat. For mild cases, that may be enough. For persistent cases, the lasting answer is a warmer wall surface, with insulation outside the building or carefully chosen insulation inside. Which one suits your home is a job for a proper survey, and we are happy to carry one out.

## Section 5: Fixing penetrating and rising damp (3:50-4:35)
On-screen text: "Find the water. Stop the water. Then repair." Checklist: render cracks, window sealant, gutters, terrace, pipes.
B-roll: Cracked render; failed sealant around a window frame; blocked downpipe; technician using a moisture meter; breathable lime plaster on an old stone wall (if available).

> For penetrating damp, find the water entry and stop it before you touch the wall finish. Common causes are cracked render, worn window and door sealant, blocked gutters and downpipes, a leaking terrace, and pipes leaking inside walls. Once the source is repaired, let the wall dry properly, which can take weeks. Only then do we replaster and repaint. That order matters. Rising damp needs care. It is often misdiagnosed, so get a proper assessment before paying for any injection or special plaster. Older stone houses usually need breathable materials, such as lime-based plaster, because a hard waterproof coating can trap moisture and push the problem elsewhere.

## Section 6: Cleaning mould and mistakes to avoid (4:35-5:10)

> Now, the mould itself. Clean it with a suitable mould remover, wear gloves and a mask, open the windows, and never mix cleaning products. Then deal with the cause, or it will come back.
